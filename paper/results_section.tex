\section{Results}

We organise the results around what they establish for model design, then
bound what they say about economic value. All comparisons are paired
$t$-tests across random seeds; where a result is described as
\emph{replicated}, it was re-run on a seed set disjoint from the one used to
discover it.

%% ===================== DESIGN FINDINGS =====================
\subsection{Graph structure improves classification, robustly}
Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation} and Table~\ref{tab:periods} report seven \emph{non-overlapping} evaluation
windows, each trained only on prior data and evaluated on the following
${\sim}15$ months. Graph structure improves per-date macro-F1 in
\textbf{7 of 7 windows} ($t=+11.67$), spanning the COVID drawdown, the 2022
bear market, and two recoveries. This is the most reproducible result in the
study and is not period-dependent.

\subsection{Draw peer groups finer than sector, but not much finer}
Figure~\ref{fig:granularity} and Table~\ref{tab:granularity} walk the graph partition down the GICS
hierarchy on \textbf{ten seeds disjoint from all other results}. Moving from
sector-level grouping to any finer partition improves tail-F1 by
${\approx}0.018$ ($p<.001$); beyond industry-group level the benefit
\emph{saturates}, with industry-group, industry and sub-industry
statistically indistinguishable. Finer partitions also use \emph{fewer}
edges, so the mechanism is grouping precision rather than connectivity.

This is the one effect in the study that is \emph{larger and more
significant on tail-F1 than on macro-F1} --- that is, on the part of the
distribution a portfolio actually uses. An earlier version of this work
reported a \emph{monotone} gradient with fineness ($+0.0090$ macro-F1,
$p<.05$, $n=5$); that did not replicate, since run-to-run nondeterminism
alone moves the comparison between $p<.05$ and non-significance. We report
the step-function result because it survives independent replication.

\paragraph{Design implication.} Use a partition around industry-group
resolution (${\sim}25$ groups for a 500-stock universe). Sector-level
grouping is measurably too coarse; going finer than industry-group buys
nothing while making the graph sparser.

\subsection{Free classifications match proprietary relationship data}
With a genuine sector graph as the comparison, a graph built from Bloomberg
supplier/customer and institutional-holder relationships is statistically
indistinguishable from one built from free GICS labels
($\Delta = +0.0033$, $t=0.82$, n.s.), while both beat no-graph ($p<.001$ and
$p<.01$ respectively). Supplier/customer edges recover ${\sim}90\%$ of the
proprietary graph's gain over no-graph; institutional-holder overlap and
rolling return-correlation graphs add nothing ($\Delta = -0.006$ and
$-0.005$, both n.s.).

An earlier version of this study reached the opposite conclusion because its
``sector proxy'' baseline was, through a labelling error, a random block
graph. Against random edges the proprietary premium appears significant
($p<.05$); against a real sector partition it vanishes. We report this
because validating relationship data against degenerate proxies is a
plausible failure mode elsewhere in this literature.

\paragraph{Design implication.} Exhaust free industry classifications before
licensing relationship data for this task.

\subsection{Hold longer: performance rises and turnover collapses}
Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} and Table~\ref{tab:horizon} report non-overlapping backtests at 5, 20 and 60
days (hold $H$ days, then trade), equal-weight quintiles, 5\,bps costs.
Absolute performance improves substantially for \emph{both} signals as the
holding period lengthens, while annualised turnover falls roughly
sevenfold --- Sharpe rises from $0.11$--$0.37$ at five days to
$0.88$--$1.49$ at sixty.

rewards, and precisely what a quintile long-short book cannot use.

%% ===================== BOUNDING ECONOMIC VALUE =====================
\subsection{Bounding the economic value}
\label{sec:bound}
Given the above, we should not expect the classification gain to become
portfolio performance, and it does not. This section bounds how strong that
negative statement can be.

Under mean-variance sizing on an adequately powered window (383 rebalances)
the graph signals underperform an identical no-graph portfolio: sub-industry
Sharpe $0.174$ vs.\ $1.117$ ($\Delta=-0.94$), Bloomberg $0.716$
($\Delta=-0.40$). The shortfall is not a transaction-cost effect --- the
per-period gross-return gap is $-44.4$\,bp against a cost gap of
$+5.0$\,bp, so costs explain ${\sim}10\%$. Realised long-short spread is
worse in six of seven windows (Table~\ref{tab:periods}).

Four candidate remedies fail. Signal shrinkage ($\lambda: 0 \to 0.95$) moves
Sharpe $0.431 \to 0.444$ (n.s.). A transaction-cost-aware turnover penalty
cuts turnover $1.489 \to 1.340$ but also Sharpe $0.438 \to 0.357$ ($p<.05$).
Aggregating to a group-level \emph{industry rotation} book --- motivated by
the between-industry identification above --- is significantly \emph{worse}
than stock selection ($-5.35$\,bp, $t=-2.78$, $p<.05$; better in 1 of 7
windows), showing that between-industry identification does not imply a
group-level portfolio. And extending the horizon, though it improves
absolute performance, does not widen the graph's advantage:
$\Delta$ Sharpe is $+0.258$ $(t{=}{+}1.99)$ at 5 days, $-0.060$
$(t{=}{-}0.32)$ at 20, and $-0.614$ $(t{=}{-}1.22)$ at 60.

\paragraph{How strong is this negative result?} Weaker than it first
appears, and we prefer to say so. The sign of the mean-variance comparison
\emph{reverses} under equal-weight sizing at the same horizon
($+0.258$ Sharpe, $t=+1.99$); consistent with
\citet{michaud1989markowitz}, equal-weight sizing of identical picks
exhibits $3.4\times$ lower seed variance than mean-variance sizing
($0.31$ vs.\ $1.07$), so mean-variance comparisons cannot establish
direction at this effect size. Statistical power also falls sharply with
holding period: non-overlapping 60-day holds leave only eight independent
periods per window--seed, so a Sharpe standard error of roughly
$1/\sqrt{8} \approx 0.35$ swamps the observed gaps. Across two sizing rules,
three horizons and a group-level construction, no comparison reaches
significance once power is accounted for.

The defensible claim is therefore narrow: a robust and replicated
classification gain yields \emph{no demonstrable} portfolio advantage here.
We do not claim the signal destroys value, and we cannot distinguish a
genuinely absent economic effect from one too small to detect with a decade
of 500-stock data.

%% ===================== ROBUSTNESS =====================
\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}
The static supply-chain snapshot induces look-ahead whose severity scales
with distance from the pull date. If contamination drove the results, the
graph advantage should shrink as evaluation approaches the snapshot; it does
the opposite, being smallest in the most-contaminated window (2017,
$+0.0096$) and largest and tightest in the cleanest (2024, $+0.0192$,
$t=+19.7$). We therefore attribute the signal to persistent relationships,
for which a stale snapshot is a serviceable proxy.

Two quantities in this study are large relative to the effects measured:
run-to-run nondeterminism, which alone flips the granularity gradient
between $p<.05$ and non-significance, and evaluation-window choice, where
single-window long-short spread estimates swing ${\sim}16$\,bp between runs
of an identical configuration. We accordingly report multi-window aggregates
and disjoint-seed replications throughout, and treat single-window or
single-seed comparisons as uninformative. We would encourage the same
standard elsewhere in this literature.
