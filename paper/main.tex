\title{\Huge \bfseries Better Rankings, Worse Portfolios:\\
Graph Neural Networks Improve a Classification Metric\\
That Portfolios Cannot Trade}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Graph neural networks for equity prediction are typically validated on
classification metrics, with proprietary relationship data --- supply-chain
links, institutional ownership --- presumed to be the highest-value input. We
test both premises on a cross-sectional 5-day forward return rank task over
S\&P 500 constituents (2015--2024), holding the architecture fixed and varying
only the graph. Graph structure improves per-date macro-F1 robustly, in seven
of seven non-overlapping evaluation windows ($t=+11.67$), and partition
granularity matters as a \emph{step} rather than a gradient: moving from
sector-level grouping to any finer partition raises tail-F1 by
${\approx}0.018$ ($p<.001$, replicated on disjoint seeds), after which the
benefit saturates by roughly 25 groups. Proprietary relationship data carries
no premium --- a graph from free GICS labels is statistically indistinguishable
from one built on Bloomberg supplier/customer and holder relationships.

None of this becomes portfolio performance. In a Markowitz mean-variance
portfolio the graph signals significantly \emph{underperform} an identical
no-graph portfolio ($\Delta=-0.94$ Sharpe, $p<.01$), the shortfall is present
gross of transaction costs, and realised long-short spread is worse in six of
seven windows. Two analyses explain the dissociation. Per-class F1 shows the
accuracy gain sits almost entirely in the Neutral class --- 60\% of the mass,
which a quintile long-short book never trades --- while tail accuracy is
flat-to-worse. Fama--MacBeth regressions show the residual return-predictive
content does not survive controls for momentum, short-term reversal,
volatility and industry fixed effects (23\% of univariate magnitude retained,
pooled $t=+0.64$), and is identified almost entirely \emph{between} industries
rather than within them. We conclude that balanced classification accuracy
rewards precisely the region of the cross-sectional distribution portfolios
cannot access, and that cross-sectional models should be selected on
tail-region metrics and realised spread rather than macro-F1.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial markets are relational systems: firms co-move because they share
macroeconomic exposures, product markets, ownership structure, and production
network dependencies. A large empirical literature documents that economic
linkages between firms predict returns beyond firm-level characteristics ---
most prominently \citet{cohen2008economic}, who show that returns of
economically linked customers predict suppliers' future returns. Recent work
models these linkages with graph neural networks, typically targeting the
relationships themselves, for example forecasting the evolution of equity
correlations \citep{fanshawe2026forecasting}.

This literature rests on two assumptions that are rarely tested together.
First, that the \emph{specificity} of relationship data matters --- that a
curated bilateral supply-chain link is worth more than a coarse statement that
two firms operate in the same industry, which is why such data is licensed at
significant cost. Second, that improvements in classification accuracy
translate into economic value. We test both, holding the model architecture
fixed and varying only the graph, and find the first unsupported and the
second inverted.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A robustly replicated classification result.} Graph structure
  over economic groupings improves cross-sectional macro-F1 in 7 of 7
  non-overlapping windows ($t=+11.67$), and finer-than-sector partitions
  improve tail-F1 by ${\approx}0.018$ ($p<.001$) on seeds disjoint from those
  used to discover the effect. The benefit is a \emph{step} that saturates by
  ${\sim}25$ groups, not the monotone gradient an earlier version of this work
  reported and which failed replication.
\item \textbf{No premium for proprietary relationship data.} Against a genuine
  sector partition, Bloomberg supplier/customer and holder graphs are
  statistically indistinguishable from free GICS labels. We document that the
  opposite conclusion arises naturally when the ``sector proxy'' baseline is
  degenerate --- in our own earlier analysis a labelling error made it a random
  block graph, against which the proprietary premium appears significant.
\item \textbf{An accuracy--tradeability dissociation, with mechanism.} The same
  signals that rank better build significantly worse mean-variance portfolios,
  gross of costs. Per-class F1 localises the gain to the untradeable Neutral
  class; Fama--MacBeth regressions show the remainder is subsumed by momentum,
  reversal and industry effects and is identified between rather than within
  industries. We test and reject three competing explanations --- transaction
  costs, signal smoothing, and portfolio concentration.
\item \textbf{A quantified warning about evaluation practice.} Run-to-run
  nondeterminism alone moves our granularity comparison between $p<.05$ and
  non-significance, and single-window long-short spread estimates swing
  ${\sim}16$\,bp between runs of an identical configuration. We report
  multi-window aggregates and disjoint-seed replications throughout.
\end{itemize}

Our reading is that a graph's value for cross-sectional ranking lies in
delimiting \emph{who is comparable to whom}, and that narrow peer groups do
this better than either broad sectors or curated bilateral links --- but that
this is group-level information, which classification metrics reward and
quintile portfolios cannot use. This complements text-based approaches to
endogenous industry classification \citep{hoberg2016text}, which similarly find
that finer, more adaptive peer definitions carry economic content coarse
classifications miss.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Data}
Daily price and volume for 500 S\&P 500 constituents (2015--2024; 1.83M
stock-days) from a Bloomberg-sourced workbook, with per-ticker reference
metadata: top suppliers, top customers, and top-20 institutional holders.
Sector assignments use GICS at four levels (Bloomberg \texttt{GICS\_*\_NAME};
469/500 tickers, with a crosswalked fallback for 19 and an explicit unknown
bucket for 12).

Three limitations shape the analysis. First, the supplier/customer/holder
metadata is a \emph{single static snapshot} taken near the end of the sample;
Bloomberg's supply-chain function does not honour historical date overrides
(verified directly), so the same graph applies to all years.
Section~\ref{sec:robust} tests whether this look-ahead drives the results.
Second, the universe is survivorship-biased: constituents were screened near
the pull date. Third, prices exclude delisting returns, and portfolio
transaction costs are a flat 5\,bps assumption.

\subsection{Task}
For each day $t$ and stock $i$ the label is the within-date rank quantile of
the forward 5-day return: Down (bottom 20\%), Neutral (middle 60\%), Up (top
20\%). Random-baseline macro-F1 is ${\approx}0.333$. This replaced an earlier
regime-classification task whose labels were mechanical functions of
observable aggregate features, so that a three-feature logistic regression
achieved AUC $0.963$ and dominated any graph model --- a label-leakage failure
documented, with two other abandoned formulations, in the project repository.

\subsection{Model}
Each trading day is a graph over stocks with up to three edge types: rolling
return correlation (top-$k$), group membership (same-GICS-group at a
configurable hierarchy level) or institutional-holder overlap (Jaccard
$\geq 0.4$), and directed supplier/customer links. The model projects 22
per-stock daily features into a hidden representation, applies two layers of
per-relation scaled-dot-product attention at each of 30 daily snapshots,
aggregates temporally with a per-stock LSTM, and classifies with a
class-weighted cross-entropy head (${\sim}1.0$M parameters; 15 epochs; AdamW
with cosine decay). Unless noted, training uses data through 2022-09-30 and
validation covers 2022Q4--2024. All comparisons are paired $t$-tests across
random seeds; results described as \emph{replicated} were re-run on a seed set
disjoint from the one used to discover them.

\subsection{Portfolio layer}
To test economic significance a Markowitz mean-variance layer converts
predictions to portfolios: expected returns $\mu_i = P(\text{Up})_i -
P(\text{Down})_i$ shrunk toward zero \citep{michaud1989markowitz}, covariance
from rolling correlations, long top-quintile / short bottom-quintile,
dollar-neutral, gross exposure capped at 2.0, rebalanced every 5 days at
5\,bps per unit turnover. We additionally evaluate a shrinkage sweep, a
transaction-cost-aware turnover penalty, and equal-weight sizing of identical
picks.

\section{Conclusion}

Holding architecture fixed and varying only the graph, we find that graph
structure over economic peer groups reliably improves cross-sectional
return-rank classification --- in every one of seven independent evaluation
windows --- and that how finely those peer groups are drawn matters, up to a
saturation point around 25 groups. Neither result requires proprietary
relationship data: free GICS classifications match Bloomberg supplier/customer
graphs on every comparison we ran.

The same models build significantly worse portfolios. The dissociation is not
a transaction-cost artifact and is not repaired by shrinkage or turnover
penalties. Per-class analysis shows the accuracy these models add sits in the
middle of the cross-sectional distribution, which a quintile long-short book
never trades, while tail accuracy is flat-to-worse; Fama--MacBeth regressions
show what predictive content remains is largely subsumed by momentum,
reversal and industry effects, and lives between industries rather than within
them. Standard balanced classification metrics therefore reward a region of
the distribution that portfolio construction cannot reach.

We draw two implications. For practice, cross-sectional equity models should be
selected on tail-region metrics and realised long-short spread rather than
macro-F1, and free industry classifications should be exhausted before
licensing relationship data. For the literature, the gap between classification
accuracy and economic value is not merely an untested assumption but, in this
setting, an inverted one --- and we would encourage reporting multi-window
aggregates and disjoint-seed replications as a matter of course, given how
large run-to-run and window-to-window variation proved to be relative to the
effects measured.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The supply-chain metadata is a
single static snapshot; a recency diagnostic indicates look-ahead does not
drive our results, but a point-in-time reconstruction from SEC customer
disclosures would close the question. The universe is survivorship-biased to
S\&P 500 constituents screened near the snapshot date, and returns exclude
delisting effects. Size and book-to-market are uncontrolled. Absolute effect
sizes are modest throughout --- macro-F1 ${\approx}0.37$ against a $0.333$
chance floor. Finally, our findings concern a 5-day horizon and a
quintile-based long-short construction; whether the dissociation persists at
longer horizons or under other portfolio rules is untested.
