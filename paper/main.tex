\title{\Huge \bfseries How to Build a Graph for Cross-Sectional\\
Equity Prediction --- and What It Will and Will Not Buy You}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Graph neural networks for equity prediction are usually validated on
classification metrics, and proprietary relationship data --- supply-chain
links, institutional ownership --- is presumed to be the highest-value input.
We hold the architecture fixed, vary only the graph, and ask what actually
matters. On a cross-sectional 5-day forward return rank task over S\&P 500
constituents (2015--2024) we establish three design results, each replicated
on seeds or periods disjoint from those used to find it. First, graph
structure over economic peer groups improves per-date macro-F1 in seven of
seven non-overlapping evaluation windows ($t=+11.67$), spanning the COVID
drawdown and the 2022 bear market. Second, peer-group resolution matters as a
\emph{step} rather than a gradient: moving from sector-level grouping to any
finer partition raises tail-F1 by ${\approx}0.018$ ($p<.001$), after which the
benefit saturates by roughly 25 groups --- and finer partitions achieve this
with \emph{fewer} edges, so the mechanism is grouping precision, not
connectivity. Third, proprietary relationship data carries no premium: a graph
from free GICS labels is statistically indistinguishable from one built on
Bloomberg supplier/customer and holder relationships. We also find that
lengthening the holding period from five to sixty days raises Sharpe three- to
thirteen-fold while cutting annualised turnover roughly sevenfold, for graph
and no-graph signals alike.

We then bound what these gains are worth. Per-class analysis shows the
classification improvement sits almost entirely in the Neutral class --- 60\%
of the mass, which a quintile long-short book never trades --- while
Fama--MacBeth regressions show the residual predictive content is largely
subsumed by momentum, reversal and industry effects and is identified
\emph{between} industries rather than within them. Consistent with this, no
portfolio construction realises the gain: a pre-registered sweep over three
holding periods and six constructions returns $\Delta$ Sharpe $=-0.032$
$(t{=}{-}0.15)$ on the specification committed to in advance, and the
comparison is governed by holding period rather than by construction ---
the graph leads in six of six constructions at five days and none of six at
twenty or sixty, while every five-day book has a negative
certainty-equivalent return at $30$--$38$ annual turns. Information measures
taken before any portfolio is formed give the mechanism: the graph model's
incremental $R^2$ over the no-graph baseline is \emph{negative} at both
twenty and sixty days, so at tradeable horizons the graph contributes noise
rather than weak signal. We remain careful about direction --- the sign of
the mean-variance comparison reverses under equal-weight sizing, power falls
sharply as the holding period lengthens, and no comparison survives
correction for multiplicity --- but the shortfall is not an artifact of the
mapping from signal to weights. The conclusion for practice is that balanced
classification accuracy rewards a region of the cross-sectional distribution
that portfolios cannot access, and that the accuracy which does reach the
tails sits at a horizon whose turnover costs exceed it --- so these models
should be selected on tail-region metrics and realised spread at the intended
holding period, never on macro-F1.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial markets are relational systems: firms co-move because they share
macroeconomic exposures, product markets, ownership structure, and production
network dependencies. A large empirical literature documents that economic
linkages between firms predict returns beyond firm-level characteristics ---
most prominently \citet{cohen2008economic}, who show that returns of
economically linked customers predict suppliers' future returns. Recent work
models these linkages with graph neural networks, typically targeting the
relationships themselves, for example forecasting the evolution of equity
correlations \citep{fanshawe2026forecasting}.

This literature rests on two assumptions that are rarely tested together.
First, that the \emph{specificity} of relationship data matters --- that a
curated bilateral supply-chain link is worth more than a coarse statement that
two firms operate in the same industry, which is why such data is licensed at
significant cost. Second, that improvements in classification accuracy
translate into economic value. We test both, holding the model architecture
fixed and varying only the graph, and find the first unsupported and the
second inverted.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Three replicated design results.} Graph structure over economic
  groupings improves classification in 7 of 7 non-overlapping windows
  ($t=+11.67$); peer-group resolution matters as a step that saturates by
  ${\sim}25$ groups ($+0.018$ tail-F1, $p<.001$, on disjoint seeds); and free
  GICS labels match proprietary supplier/customer and holder graphs. Each
  yields concrete guidance: build the graph at industry-group resolution, and
  do not license relationship data for this task.
\item \textbf{Holding period dominates graph choice.} Lengthening the hold
  from 5 to 60 days raises Sharpe three- to thirteen-fold and cuts annualised
  turnover ${\sim}7\times$, for graph and no-graph signals alike --- a larger
  effect than any graph-design choice we tested.
\item \textbf{A characterisation of what the graph confers.} Per-class F1 and
  Fama--MacBeth regressions jointly locate the gain as group-level and
  middle-of-distribution: concentrated in the Neutral class, identified
  between rather than within industries, and largely subsumed by momentum,
  reversal and industry effects.
\item \textbf{A bounded negative result on tradeability.} No construction we
  tested --- two sizing rules, three horizons, and a group-level rotation book
  --- realises the classification gain as portfolio performance. We report
  this with explicit limits: the mean-variance sign reverses under
  equal-weight sizing, and power collapses at long horizons, so we
  distinguish ``no demonstrable advantage'' from ``value destroyed''.
\item \textbf{A quantified warning about evaluation practice.} Run-to-run
  nondeterminism alone moves our granularity comparison between $p<.05$ and
  non-significance, and single-window spread estimates swing ${\sim}16$\,bp
  between runs of an identical configuration. We report multi-window
  aggregates and disjoint-seed replications throughout.
\end{itemize}

Our reading is that a graph's value for cross-sectional ranking lies in
delimiting \emph{who is comparable to whom}, and that narrow peer groups do
this better than either broad sectors or curated bilateral links --- but that
this is group-level information, which classification metrics reward and
quintile portfolios cannot use. This complements text-based approaches to
endogenous industry classification \citep{hoberg2016text}, which similarly find
that finer, more adaptive peer definitions carry economic content coarse
classifications miss.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Economic links and cross-predictability.} That inter-firm linkages
carry return-predictive information is well established.
\citet{cohen2008economic} show customer returns predict supplier returns;
\citet{menzly2010market} obtain cross-predictability from supplier--customer
industry pairs; \citet{hoberg2016text} construct product-market networks from
10-K text; and \citet{lee2019technological} do the same from patent
similarity. \citet{hou2007industry} attributes much of the lead--lag structure
in returns to slow information diffusion within industries. This literature
establishes that links matter, and is generally read as implying that
\emph{better-identified} links matter more --- which is the premise behind
licensing bilateral relationship data. We test that implication directly by
holding the architecture fixed and varying only the graph, and do not find it:
a free classification-derived graph matches a proprietary
supplier/customer-and-holder graph on every seed.

\paragraph{Industry classification as a design choice.} A separate literature
compares classification schemes on their ability to explain comovement and
fundamentals. \citet{bhojraj2003whats} evaluate SIC, NAICS, GICS and
Fama--French groupings for capital-market research and find GICS strongest;
\citet{chan2007industry} study how classification affects measured return
comovement. \citet{moskowitz1999industries} show industry effects account for
much of what is read as individual-stock momentum. We take the next step and
treat classification \emph{resolution} as a tunable graph-construction
parameter rather than a fixed choice, which surfaces a result these papers do
not address: the benefit is a step that saturates near 25 groups, and it is
realised with fewer edges rather than more.

\paragraph{Graph neural networks for equity prediction.} A now-substantial
body of work applies graph learning to stock prediction, differing mainly in
the relation source and the temporal encoder ---
\citet{chen2018incorporating} using shareholder links,
\citet{feng2019temporal} adding a ranking objective over sector and
supply-chain relations, \citet{kim2019hats} using hierarchical attention over
multiple relation types, and \citet{sawhney2021exploring} exploiting
scale-free structure. These papers report accuracy, ranking or return metrics
and generally treat an improvement in them as the result. Our contribution is
orthogonal to architecture: we ask which part of the predictive distribution
the improvement occupies, and find it concentrated in the untraded middle. The
mechanism we identify --- performance improving as the graph becomes sparser
but more precisely grouped --- is consistent with the graph-learning findings
that homophily governs when message passing helps
\citep{zhu2020beyond} and that excessive smoothing degrades node
discrimination \citep{li2018deeper}.

\paragraph{Machine learning in asset pricing, and evaluation credibility.}
\citet{gu2020empirical} establish the benchmark for ML in cross-sectional
return prediction and the practice of evaluating on economic rather than
purely statistical criteria. Our negative result is best read against three
strands of evidence on why reported edges fail to survive:
\citet{novymarx2016taxonomy} and \citet{korajczyk2004momentum} on trading
costs consuming high-turnover anomalies, which is precisely the channel that
disqualifies our five-day effect; \citet{harvey2016cross} and
\citet{mclean2016does} on multiplicity and post-publication decay, which
motivates our pre-committing a primary specification before sweeping an
18-cell grid; and \citet{bailey2014pseudo} on backtest overfitting. On the
portfolio side, \citet{michaud1989markowitz} identifies the error-maximisation
property of mean-variance optimisation \citep{markowitz1952portfolio} that
makes our sizing comparison underpowered, and \citet{demiguel2009optimal}
explain why the equal-weight book we compare against is a demanding rather
than a naive benchmark.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Data}
Daily price and volume for 500 S\&P 500 constituents (2015--2024; 1.83M
stock-days) from a Bloomberg-sourced workbook, with per-ticker reference
metadata: top suppliers, top customers, and top-20 institutional holders.
Sector assignments use GICS at four levels (Bloomberg \texttt{GICS\_*\_NAME};
469/500 tickers, with a crosswalked fallback for 19 and an explicit unknown
bucket for 12).

Three limitations shape the analysis. First, the supplier/customer/holder
metadata is a \emph{single static snapshot} taken near the end of the sample;
Bloomberg's supply-chain function does not honour historical date overrides
(verified directly), so the same graph applies to all years.
Section~\ref{sec:robust} tests whether this look-ahead drives the results.
Second, the universe is survivorship-biased: constituents were screened near
the pull date. Third, prices exclude delisting returns, and portfolio
transaction costs are a flat 5\,bps assumption.

\subsection{Task}
For each day $t$ and stock $i$ the label is the within-date rank quantile of
the forward 5-day return: Down (bottom 20\%), Neutral (middle 60\%), Up (top
20\%). Random-baseline macro-F1 is ${\approx}0.333$. This replaced an earlier
regime-classification task whose labels were mechanical functions of
observable aggregate features, so that a three-feature logistic regression
achieved AUC $0.963$ and dominated any graph model --- a label-leakage failure
documented, with two other abandoned formulations, in the project repository.

\subsection{Model}
Each trading day is a graph over stocks with up to three edge types: rolling
return correlation (top-$k$), group membership (same-GICS-group at a
configurable hierarchy level) or institutional-holder overlap (Jaccard
$\geq 0.4$), and directed supplier/customer links. The model projects 22
per-stock daily features into a hidden representation, applies two layers of
per-relation scaled-dot-product attention \citep{vaswani2017attention,
velickovic2018graph} at each of 30 daily snapshots, aggregates temporally with
a per-stock LSTM \citep{hochreiter1997long}, and classifies with a
class-weighted cross-entropy head (${\sim}1.0$M parameters; 15 epochs; AdamW
with cosine decay). Unless noted, training uses data through 2022-09-30 and
validation covers 2022Q4--2024. All comparisons are paired $t$-tests across
random seeds; results described as \emph{replicated} were re-run on a seed set
disjoint from the one used to discover them.

\subsection{Portfolio layer}
To test economic significance a mean-variance layer \citep{markowitz1952portfolio}
converts predictions to portfolios: expected returns $\mu_i = P(\text{Up})_i -
P(\text{Down})_i$ shrunk toward zero \citep{michaud1989markowitz}, covariance
from rolling correlations with ridge regularisation in the spirit of
\citet{ledoit2004wellconditioned}, long top-quintile / short bottom-quintile,
dollar-neutral, gross exposure capped at 2.0, rebalanced every 5 days at
5\,bps per unit turnover. We additionally evaluate a shrinkage sweep, a
transaction-cost-aware turnover penalty, and equal-weight sizing of identical
picks --- the last motivated by \citet{demiguel2009optimal}, who show naive
diversification is hard to beat once estimation error is priced in.

\section{Conclusion}

Holding the architecture fixed and varying only the graph, three design
results replicate on independent seeds or periods. Graph structure over
economic peer groups improves cross-sectional return-rank classification in
every one of seven independent windows. Peer-group resolution matters, as a
step that saturates around industry-group level rather than a gradient, and
finer partitions achieve this with fewer edges --- so what a graph buys is
precision in defining who is comparable to whom, not connectivity. And none
of it requires proprietary relationship data: free GICS classifications match
Bloomberg supplier/customer graphs on every comparison we ran. Separately, and
with a larger effect than any graph-design choice, lengthening the holding
period from five to sixty days raises Sharpe substantially while cutting
turnover by an order of magnitude.

What these gains are worth is a different question. Per-class analysis shows
the accuracy the graph adds sits in the middle of the cross-sectional
distribution, which a quintile long-short book never trades, while
Fama--MacBeth regressions show the remainder is largely subsumed by momentum,
reversal and industry effects and lives between industries rather than within
them. Consistent with that characterisation, no portfolio construction we
tested realises the gain.

We are deliberately careful about how strong that negative claim can be. The
mean-variance comparison favours no-graph, but its sign reverses under
equal-weight sizing, and no construction --- across two sizing rules, three
horizons, and a group-level rotation book --- reaches significance once power
is accounted for. The defensible statement is that a robust and replicated
classification gain does not translate into measurable economic value here,
not that the signal destroys value.

The practical implication is a change of objective. Balanced classification
accuracy rewards precisely the region of the distribution portfolios cannot
access, so cross-sectional equity models should be selected on tail-region
metrics and realised long-short spread. Given how large run-to-run and
window-to-window variation proved to be relative to the effects measured, we
would also encourage reporting multi-window aggregates and disjoint-seed
replications as a matter of course.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The supply-chain metadata is a
single static snapshot; a recency diagnostic indicates look-ahead does not
drive our results, but a point-in-time reconstruction from SEC customer
disclosures would close the question. The universe is survivorship-biased to
S\&P 500 constituents screened near the snapshot date, and returns exclude
delisting effects. Size and book-to-market are uncontrolled. Absolute effect
sizes are modest throughout --- macro-F1 ${\approx}0.37$ against a $0.333$
chance floor. Finally, statistical power falls sharply as the holding period
lengthens: non-overlapping 60-day holds leave only eight independent periods
per window--seed. Distinguishing a genuinely absent economic effect from one
too small to detect would require a longer sample or a broader universe.
